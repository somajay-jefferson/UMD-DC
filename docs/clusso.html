Algorithm one

CLUSSO

Each patient arrives as a matrix, not a vector: one row per tubule segmented from their biopsy slide, one column per image feature. The row count varies wildly between patients — a median near 929 tubules, interquartile range roughly 570 to 1,377. The outcome being predicted is a single number, eGFR, a measure of kidney function.

That shape breaks every existing scalar‑on‑matrix method, which requires the same matrix dimensions for everyone. Two structural problems, actually: the matrices are unbalanced, and their rows are unordered — tubule 7 in one patient has nothing to do with tubule 7 in another. Clustering fixes both at once, and preserves heterogeneity that plain averaging would destroy.

{ Xi ∈ ℝpi×q , yi ∈ ℝ : i=1,…,n }

Rows vary in number and carry no shared ordering. Columns are the same 57 features for everyone.

  1. 1

    Pool

    Every tubule from every patient goes into one matrix. Not per‑patient — the clustering has to be global so that “cluster 1” means the same thing for everyone.

    𝕋∈ ℝ (∑ipi) ×q

    Roughly 225,000 × 57 for the NEPTUNE cohort.

  2. 2

    Cluster

    Gaussian mixture model with G=2. Every tubule gets a label — nominally atrophic versus non‑atrophic, though the paper only formally assumes a two‑component latent mixture.

    Tji ⟶ label ∈ {1,2}

    Unsupervised. This step never sees the outcome.

  3. 3

    Average and reweight

    Collapse within patient and within cluster. The weights are each patient’s share of tubules per cluster — which matters because the proportion of atrophic tubules is itself the known prognostic signal.

    X^ i,k ∗ = ∑j=1pi 𝟙(Tji∈𝕋^k) Tji ∑j=1pi 𝟙(Tji∈𝕋^k)

    Row k of the cluster‑averaged matrix: the mean of patient i’s tubules assigned to cluster k.

    w^ki = 1pi ∑j=1pi 𝟙(Tji∈𝕋^k)

    The observed share of that patient’s tubules landing in cluster k.

    X^ i ∗∗ = diag ( w^1i , w^2i ) X^ i ∗ ∈ ℝ2×q

    Balanced at last — two rows for every patient, regardless of tubule count.

    This is the payoff. Averaging across all tubules would flatten a genuinely bimodal distribution — atrophic tubules have thickened basement membranes — producing a number that describes neither population.
  4. 4

    Structured lasso

    Two coefficient vectors instead of one: α for rows (clusters), β for columns (features). Solved by alternating, because the objective is bilinear and therefore non‑convex.

    yi= α⊤ X^i∗∗ β + εi
    arg min 1n ∑i=1n ( yi− α⊤ X^i∗∗ β ) 2 + λn ‖β‖ 1

    Subject to ‖α‖1=1 and a fixed sign on the largest component of β, for identifiability.

    repeat up to 50 times
    fix α: Z=α⊤X^∗∗ n × q
    β←lasso(Z,y;λ)
    fix β: W=X^∗∗β n × 2
    α←OLS(W,y)
    until ‖ β⊗α − β0⊗α0 ‖ <0.01
    λ chosen by 5‑fold cross‑validation non-convex → 5 random restarts, keep best objective
    Only β is penalized. The paper deliberately departs from Zhao & Leng here — both clusters must stay in the model, so α is left unregularized. The selected set is always about features, never clusters.
S= { j : β^j ≠0 }

The selected feature set — the only thing PS‑Fdr will need.

Twelve patients, three features, start to finish

A worked example

Everything below was produced by running the repository’s own estimator — Mainfunction_albet.py and its glmnet‑matching lasso — on a cohort small enough to print in full. Two tubule populations, normal at roughly (2, 10) and atrophic at (6, 2), with measurement noise σR=0.3 added to every tubule.

The third feature is the interesting one. Call it nuclei per tubule: it varies from patient to patient but is identical in both tubule populations and has nothing to do with the outcome. It is a pure nuisance, planted so we can watch whether the lasso kills it.

α∗= (1,3) β∗= (2,−1,0) σ=0.5

The truth we are trying to recover. Atrophic tubules count three times as much; feature 3 has no effect at all.

Follow one row across. Patient A’s four normal tubules average to (1.90, 10.03, 4.91). Multiply by w₁ = 0.80 and you get row 1, (1.52, 8.03, 3.93). That is the only place the weights are ever used — once, here, and then they are absorbed into the numbers. Every figure here is displayed to two decimals while the arithmetic runs at full precision — so 0.80 × 10.03 reads 8.02 by hand, where the row shows 8.03 from the unrounded 10.0334.
Now read the third number in the two cluster-mean columns. 4.91 against 4.92. 6.66 against 6.66. 5.07 against 5.07. The nuisance feature is the same in both populations for every single patient — it carries no cluster structure whatsoever, which is exactly why it deserves to be dropped. Compare that to features 1 and 2, which separate cleanly around 1.9 against 5.9 and 10.0 against 2.0.
The last two design columns are one column. row₁ + row₂ equals the naive average for every patient, to machine precision — largest discrepancy across all 12 patients and 3 features is 1.78e‑15. The rows partition the naive vector; that is the identity, not a coincidence.
yi= (α∗)⊤ X^i∗∗ β∗ +εi εi∼ N(0,0.52)

The last column, y, is the outcome being predicted — this toy’s stand-in for eGFR. It is the target, not part of the design. For patient A: row₁ + 3·row₂ = (5.12, 9.28, 6.88), dotted with β∗=(2,−1,0) gives 0.96, and the observed 0.20 is that plus a noise draw of −0.76. Feature 3 contributes nothing, because β3∗=0.

Sort that column by w₂ and it climbs almost monotonically — 0.20 at 20% atrophic, 24.20 at 83%. Not luck: with α∗=(1,3) the atrophic row counts triple, which is the simulated form of “percentage of tubules with atrophy predicts worse kidney function.”
⋅ Inside the lasso — standardizing before the penalty

Before any penalty is applied, Z=α⊤X^∗∗ gets column‑wise z‑scored across the twelve patients. The raw columns differ in spread by more than a factor of two, and an L1 penalty is not scale‑invariant — without this, lambda would silently punish the narrow column hardest.

Read the middle column downward and lambda is revealed for what it is: a dial. Turn it up and false discoveries fall away; turn it up further and real features start disappearing too. The naive lasso sits at 57.5% false positives — matched exactly by CLUSSO at λ=0.10, where CLUSSO is the weaker method. Only by accepting a much higher miss rate does CLUSSO buy its lower false positive rate here.

That is not the paper’s finding, and the gap is worth naming: twelve patients and three features is orders of magnitude smaller than the 300 to 2,000 subjects and 10 to 200 features the simulation study uses, and CLUSSO’s advantage is reported to grow with the number of features. A toy this size demonstrates the mechanism, not the comparative performance.

But look at what the dial implies. Somebody has to choose a point on that curve, and cross‑validation chooses it by minimizing prediction error — a criterion with no relationship whatsoever to how many of the selected features are real. Nothing in the table lets you say “give me a set that is at most 10% false.”

That sentence is the entire reason the second algorithm exists.

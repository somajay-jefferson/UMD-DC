Algorithm two

PS‑Fdr

Cross‑validated lasso does not control false discoveries. He et al. demonstrate this brutally: 340 patients, 23,052 genes, 50 of which truly matter — and across 10,000 repetitions the false discovery proportion clusters around 0.90. Nine in ten “findings” are noise.

The fix cannot use p‑values, because penalized estimators have no tractable limiting distribution, and it cannot use naive permutation, because under a global null the noise variables face less competition for the L1 budget than they do in real data — so the false discovery count comes out too low. What follows is the workaround.

FDP= N0N+ FDR=E(FDP) Fdr^= e^0N+

Falsely selected over total selected; its expectation; and the estimator the algorithm actually computes.

  1. 1

    Stability selection real data

    Rather than trusting one CV‑tuned fit, ask how reliably each variable gets picked across resamples.

    for b=1,…,B B = 50
    resample n rows with replacement
    fit lasso ⟶ S(b) ={j: β^j(b) ≠0} call site A
    Πj= 1B ∑b=1B 𝟙(j∈ S(b) ) Π(1) ≤⋯≤ Π(p)

    Selection frequency, then sorted into order statistics.

  2. 2

    Permutation null calibration

    Shuffle the outcome to sever every association, then run the whole of step 1 again. Nested loops — this is where the compute lives.

    for m=1,…,M M = 100
    permute y kills all y–X association
    for b=1,…,B
    resample, fit lasso call site B
    fix the number selected to the median from step 1 — do not re‑run CV
    Π―(j) = 1M ∑m=1M Π~(j)(m)

    Averaged over ranks, not over variable identities — the subscript is parenthesised.

    That fixed count is the whole trick. Letting CV choose freely on permuted data is exactly what makes the expected false discovery count collapse and the procedure over‑select.
  3. 3

    Normalize

    Borrowed from Significance Analysis of Microarrays. Puts the real and null arms on a comparable footing and spreads informative variables away from uninformative ones.

    D(u)= u u(1−u) +ν ν=1B
    Z(j) = D(Π(j)) Z―(j) = D(Π―(j))

    Real arm on the left, null arm on the right.

  4. 4

    Step down and cut

    Walk the ordered statistics until the real arm separates from the null arm by a margin, then count how many null values would have cleared the same bar.

    Z(Δ)= min { Z(j) : Z(j) ≥ Z―(j) +Δ }
    N+(Δ) = ∑j=1p 𝟙( Z(j) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many real variables clear the cutoff — the denominator.

    e^0(Δ) = 1M ∑j=1p ∑m=1M 𝟙( Z~(j)(m) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many null variables would have cleared it — the numerator.

    S^q = {j: Fdr^ (Δ(j)) ≤q }

    At q=0.1: of 10 selected, at most about 1 is expected to be a false positive.

Seventy subjects, thirty features, every stage shown

A worked example

Everything below was produced by running this repository’s own implementation, ps_fdr.py, on a problem small enough to print. There are seventy subjects and thirty features, and five of the features carry signal. Their coefficients descend from 1.6 to 0.4, so the last one is deliberately faint. Neighbouring features are correlated at 0.35, and the outcome noise is σ=1.5, which leaves the signal accounting for 78% of the variance in y.

The design matrix is handed to the algorithm from outside, fixed, exactly as the published method assumes. That assumption is the one the composition has to give up.

β∗= (1.6,1.2,0.9,0.6,0.4,0,…,0) n=70 p=30 B=50 M=100

Features 1 to 5 are real, the other twenty-five are noise. Nothing downstream of this line is told which is which.

0 The baseline: one cross‑validated lasso

Before any of the machinery, here is what the standard approach returns on this cohort. A single lasso, tuned by ten‑fold cross‑validation on the full data, keeping every variable with a non‑zero coefficient.

Nothing is wrong with that fit. It finds every real feature, and it was tuned to do the one thing cross‑validation optimizes, which is predict. It simply answers a different question from the one being asked. Two thirds of what it reports as a finding is noise, and the fit itself gives no way of knowing which two thirds. At the paper’s scale — 340 patients, 23,052 genes — the same figure is about 0.90.
1 Resample fifty times and count

Draw seventy subjects with replacement, fit a CV‑tuned lasso, write down which features survived, and repeat fifty times. The bar for each feature is the fraction of those fifty fits that kept it.

real feature noise feature every row is one resample · every mark is a feature that survived it
This grid is the whole of step 1. Fifty fits, thirty features, and one mark wherever a fit kept a feature. Nothing else about those fits is retained — not the coefficients, not the fitted lambda, not the prediction error. The bars are the column totals divided by fifty, and they are the only thing the rest of the procedure reads.
The ordering is already most of the answer. Features 1 to 4 were selected in all fifty resamples. But feature 5, the faint real one, sits at 0.90 — and so does feature 30, which is pure noise. Every other noise feature trails below. Stability alone ranks well and cuts nowhere; the rest of the algorithm exists to place the cut.
The support sizes are also recorded here. Across the fifty resamples the CV‑tuned lasso kept as few as 8 features and as many as all 30, with a median of 18. That median is carried into the next step and never re‑derived.
2 – 3 Build the null, then put both arms on one scale

Shuffle y, run all fifty resamples again, and do that a hundred times over — five thousand further fits. On every one of them the support size is pinned at 18. The permuted frequencies are then sorted within each permutation and averaged position by position, so the null describes what the j‑th largest selection frequency looks like when nothing is real.

Both arms then pass through D(u) with ν=1/50=0.02. It is a strictly increasing map, so it reorders nothing; it stretches the top of the scale apart. A feature selected in every single resample has u=1, where the square root vanishes and D=1/ν=50.

The real curve leaves the band at the left and rejoins it around rank 14. Where it sits inside the band, a feature is doing nothing that shuffled data would not do by chance.

Read the null column downward. Even with no signal whatever, the top‑ranked feature is selected in 94% of resamples. That is what forcing 18 selections out of 30 does, and it is the number a raw selection frequency has to beat before it means anything. Beating 0.94 by standing at 1.00 is a real gap; standing at 0.60 against a null of 0.60 is not.
Ties get ranks anyway. Features 1 to 4 all sit at exactly 1.00, so which of them occupies rank 27 and which rank 30 is arbitrary. Their null counterparts are not tied, which is why one column reads 1.00 four times over while the gap beside it runs from 46.46 to 48.00. The step‑down rule takes a minimum over these, so the arbitrariness does not reach the answer.
4 Raise the margin until the estimate clears the target

Each value of Δ demands a wider margin over the null, which fixes a cutoff, which fixes a selected set. The numerator counts how many of the three thousand permuted statistics — thirty ranks by a hundred permutations — would have cleared that same cutoff, divided by the hundred permutations.

This is the comparison the rule actually makes. One point per rank: what the real data produced against what the permutations produced at that same rank. The dashed diagonal is where the two agree. The bottom of the range sits below it — those features are selected less often in the real data than shuffled data manages by chance. Points drift above the line through the middle, and only four break away, which is why the cutoff lands where it does rather than somewhere in the crowd.

The first row estimates 14.23 false discoveries out of 14 selections. An Fdr^ above one is not a bug: the estimator assumes every variable could be null, following Benjamini and Hochberg’s convention, so it is an upper bound rather than an estimate. That row is also almost exactly the CV‑tuned lasso’s answer — fourteen features, two thirds of them noise.
There is no row with five selections, and that is where the real feature is lost. Feature 5 and noise feature 30 are tied at 0.90, so no cutoff exists that admits one without the other. Taking both means Fdr^ = 0.292, which fails at q=0.1. The procedure declines, and stops at four.

⋅ What the fixed count is worth

Step 2 pins the permuted support size at 18 rather than letting cross‑validation choose it, and the claim attached to that line is that the alternative under‑counts false discoveries. The invalid variant looks better and is worse. Free to re‑tune on permuted data, cross‑validation settles on smaller supports, the null frequencies fall, and every estimate of the false discovery count shrinks with them. Six features clear a bar that reads 0.092 — comfortably inside the target — and one of the six is noise, so the realized proportion is 0.167. It found the fifth real feature, which the published version misses. It also broke the only promise the procedure exists to make.

One cohort could be one unlucky draw, so both rules were run against each other on 40 fresh cohorts under identical settings.

Roughly double the level asked for, at every level. Ask the invalid variant for 10% and it delivers 17.8%, holding its promise on fewer than half the cohorts. The published rule delivers 0.4% and holds on 39 of 40. The trade is legible in the power column — 0.875 against 0.690 — and that is exactly the trade the paper refuses: a procedure that beats the alternative on power while failing the guarantee it is named after is not a better procedure, it is a differently‑tuned lasso with a number attached to it.
Both arms here use M = 30 rather than 100, and 40 cohorts rather than 200, so that the free‑CV variant — which re‑runs a full cross‑validation on every permuted resample instead of walking a path — finished in minutes rather than hours. Read the two halves against each other rather than against the 200‑cohort table below: the pinned arm’s power lands in the same place at either setting, 0.690 here against 0.699 there, while 40 cohorts is few enough that the last column moves by several points on resampling alone.
? Does this hold across repeated runs?

A single cohort proves nothing. The whole pipeline was therefore re‑run on 200 fresh cohorts, with a new design matrix, new outcome noise, new bootstraps and new permutations each time. That is a little over a million lasso fits. The column to watch is not the mean false discovery proportion but the last one: the share of individual cohorts on which the realized proportion actually came in at or under the level that was asked for.

The guarantee holds, and it holds with room to spare. Asked for 10%, the procedure delivers 2.6% on average. That gap is not luck: the estimator assumes every variable could be null, so Fdr^ is an upper bound and the procedure stops earlier than it strictly needs to. The cost is in the power column. At q=0.05 fewer than half the real features survive; the two weakest are usually gone.

Set against that, the CV‑tuned lasso finds 96% of the real features and reports about eleven, of which roughly five are noise. It clears a 10% false discovery proportion on one cohort in twenty. Neither column makes one procedure better than the other. They answer different questions, and only one of them lets you state the question in advance.

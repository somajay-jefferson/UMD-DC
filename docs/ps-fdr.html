Algorithm two

PS‑Fdr

Cross‑validated lasso does not control false discoveries. He et al. demonstrate this brutally: 340 patients, 23,052 genes, 50 of which truly matter — and across 10,000 repetitions the false discovery proportion clusters around 0.90. Nine in ten “findings” are noise.

The goal is the familiar one. Benjamini and Hochberg do not promise zero false positives; they promise that a known fraction of what you report is false, and let you choose the fraction. PS‑Fdr wants that same guarantee. What it gives up is the usual route to it — there is no p‑value anywhere below, and nothing is assumed about the sampling distribution of β^. The only raw material is how often a variable survives resampling, and that is available for any selection rule at all.

It has to be given up, because the fix cannot use p‑values — penalized estimators have no tractable limiting distribution — and it cannot use naive permutation either, because under a global null the noise variables face less competition for the L1 budget than they do in real data, so the false discovery count comes out too low. What follows is the workaround.

  1. 1

    Stability selection real data

    Rather than trusting one CV‑tuned fit, ask how reliably each variable gets picked across resamples.

    for b=1,…,B B = 50
    resample n rows with replacement
    fit lasso ⟶ S(b) ={j: β^j(b) ≠0} call site A
    Πj= 1B ∑b=1B 𝟙(j∈ S(b) ) Π(1) ≤⋯≤ Π(p)

    Selection frequency, then sorted into order statistics.

  2. 2

    Permutation null calibration

    Shuffle the outcome to sever every association, then run the whole of step 1 again. Nested loops — this is where the compute lives.

    for m=1,…,M M = 100
    permute y kills all y–X association
    for b=1,…,B
    resample, fit lasso call site B
    fix the number selected to the median from step 1 — do not re‑run CV
    Π―(j) = 1M ∑m=1M Π~(j)(m)

    Averaged over ranks, not over variable identities. Once y is shuffled, “feature 12 was picked often” means nothing — the labels no longer attach to anything. What is stable across shuffles is the shape: how high the most‑selected feature gets when nothing is real, how high the second, and so on. So each permutation is sorted before anything is averaged, which is what the parenthesised subscript records.

    That fixed count is the whole trick. Letting CV choose freely on permuted data is exactly what makes the expected false discovery count collapse and the procedure over‑select.
  3. 3

    Normalize

    Borrowed from Significance Analysis of Microarrays. Divides each selection frequency by its own sampling noise — the standard‑error correction that turns a raw proportion into a signal‑to‑noise ratio.

    D(u)= u u(1−u) +ν ν=1B

    The square root on the bottom is how much a fraction out of B wobbles. It is biggest at u=0.5 and shrinks to nothing as u approaches 1, which matches the fact that 25 out of 50 is a jumpy number and 50 out of 50 is a steady one. Dividing by it puts every frequency in the same units, so a gap means the same thing wherever it sits. ν is there because at u=1 that square root is zero and you cannot divide by zero. Setting it to 1/B says: with B resamples you can never measure anything finer than one resample, so do not pretend to. That also puts a ceiling on the whole thing at D(1)=B. Run more resamples and the ceiling goes up with them.

    Z(j) = D(Π(j)) Z―(j) = D(Π―(j))

    Real data on the left, shuffled data on the right. Same formula both times.

  4. 4

    Step down and cut

    Raise the required margin until the real numbers pull away from the shuffled ones, then count how many shuffled values would have cleared the same bar.

    Z(Δ)= min { Z(j) : Z(j) ≥ Z―(j) +Δ }
    N+(Δ) = ∑j=1p 𝟙( Z(j) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many real variables clear the cutoff — the denominator.

    e^0(Δ) = 1M ∑j=1p ∑m=1M 𝟙( Z~(j)(m) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many null variables would have cleared it — the numerator.

    S^q = {j: Fdr^ (Δ(j)) ≤q }

    At q=0.1: of 10 selected, at most about 1 is expected to be a false positive.

A worked example

Everything below was produced by running this repository’s own implementation, ps_fdr.py, on a problem small enough to print. There are seventy subjects and thirty features, and five of the features carry signal. Their coefficients descend from 1.6 to 0.4, so the last one is deliberately faint. Neighbouring features are correlated at 0.35, and the outcome noise is σ=1.5, which leaves the signal accounting for 78% of the variance in y.

The design matrix is handed to the algorithm from outside, fixed, exactly as the published method assumes. That assumption is the one the composition has to give up.

β∗= (1.6,1.2,0.9,0.6,0.4,0,…,0) n=70 p=30 B=50 M=100

Features 1 to 5 are real, the other twenty-five are noise. Nothing downstream of this line is told which is which.

Nothing is wrong with that fit. It finds every real feature, and it was tuned to do the one thing cross‑validation optimizes, which is predict. It simply answers a different question from the one being asked. Two thirds of what it reports as a finding is noise, and the fit itself gives no way of knowing which two thirds. At the paper’s scale — 340 patients, 23,052 genes — the same figure is about 0.90.
1 Resample fifty times and count

Draw seventy subjects with replacement, fit a CV‑tuned lasso, write down which features survived, and repeat fifty times. The bar for each feature is the fraction of those fifty fits that kept it.

real feature noise feature every row is one resample · every mark is a feature that survived it
This grid is the whole of step 1. Fifty fits, thirty features, and one mark wherever a fit kept a feature. Nothing else about those fits is retained — not the coefficients, not the fitted lambda, not the prediction error. The bars are the column totals divided by fifty, and they are the only thing the rest of the procedure reads.
The ordering is already most of the answer. Features 1 to 4 were selected in all fifty resamples. But feature 5, the faint real one, sits at 0.90 — and so does feature 30, which is pure noise. Every other noise feature trails below. Stability alone ranks well and cuts nowhere; the rest of the algorithm exists to place the cut.
The support sizes are also recorded here. Across the fifty resamples the CV‑tuned lasso kept as few as 8 features and as many as all 30, with a median of 18. That median is carried into the next step and never re‑derived.
2 – 3 Do it all again on nonsense, then put both lists in the same units

Now run step 1 a second time, on data where nothing is real. Shuffle y so no feature has any genuine link to the outcome, then do all fifty resamples again. Then shuffle and do it again, a hundred times over — five thousand more fits. Every one of them is forced to keep exactly 18 features, the median from step 1.

One catch. Once y is shuffled, the feature labels mean nothing — “feature 12 got picked a lot” is an accident of that particular shuffle. What does hold up across shuffles is the shape: how high the most‑picked feature gets when nothing is real, how high the second, and so on. So within each shuffle the thirty numbers are sorted before anything is averaged. From here on, a row of the shuffled results means a position in the ranking, not a feature.

one permutation their mean the real data
Each faint line is one shuffled dataset run through the whole of step 1. They are sorted, so a line reads “the most picked feature, the second most, and so on” — not any particular feature. The real curve leaves the band on the left and rejoins it around rank 14. Where it sits inside the band, a feature is doing nothing that shuffled data would not do by luck.

Now you have two lists of thirty numbers. Real, and shuffled. To compare them, both go through D(u), with ν=1/50=0.02.

Here is what that is for. These numbers are fractions out of fifty, and fractions out of fifty wobble by different amounts depending on where they sit. A feature picked 25 times out of 50 might come back 22 or 28 if you ran the whole thing again. A feature picked all 50 times comes back 49 or 50. Much steadier.

So a gap of 0.06 is not one thing. Down in the middle it is nothing; up at the top it is enormous. D divides each number by how much it wobbles, which puts everything in the same units so gaps are comparable wherever they sit. It never changes the order — the most‑picked feature is still the most‑picked feature. You need this because step 4 uses one single threshold across all thirty positions at once, and one threshold cannot be fair unless the numbers mean the same thing everywhere. The two halves of a row are not about the same thing, and that is on purpose. The left number belongs to a feature. The right number does not — it is what shuffled data reaches at that position in the ranking. So on the top row, 0.936 is not “feature 4 under shuffling.” It is “the highest of the thirty, under shuffling.” Feature 4 earned rank 30 on the real data, so 0.936 is the bar it has to beat.
Read the shuffled column downward and you see why any of this is needed. With no signal at all, the top‑ranked feature still gets picked 94% of the time. That is just what forcing 18 picks out of 30 does. So 0.94 is the number a raw frequency has to beat before it means anything. Standing at 1.00 against 0.94 is a real gap. Standing at 0.60 against 0.60 is not.
Rows 26 and 25 are where the whole thing gets decided. Feature 5 is real. Feature 30 is noise. Both were picked in exactly 45 of the 50 resamples, so both read 0.90, and no threshold anywhere can let one in without the other. Step 4 will end up refusing both, which costs a real feature. Nothing later in the algorithm can undo a tie that happened here.
Ties still get ranks. Features 1 to 4 all sit at exactly 1.00, so which one gets called rank 27 and which rank 30 is arbitrary. Their shuffled counterparts are not tied, which is why the real column reads 1.00 four times over while the gap beside it drifts from 46.46 to 48.00. Step 4 takes the smallest of these, so the arbitrariness never reaches the answer.

Follow the strands downward. Before D, the four features stuck at 1.00 sit a hand’s width from feature 5 and from noise feature 30. After it, they are miles away, while the crowd below 0.7 stays bunched together. The shuffled numbers went through the identical formula, so this is not a thumb on the scale — it is the transform refusing to treat 0.90 and 1.00 as neighbours, which they are not.
4 Raise the margin until the estimate clears the target

Pick a margin — how far a feature has to beat the shuffled number at its rank. That decides where the cutoff lands, which decides which features get reported. Then ask the question the whole method is built around: if that same cutoff had been applied to the shuffled data, how many features would have got through? You can just count, because you have 3,000 shuffled numbers sitting there — thirty ranks times a hundred shuffles. Divide the count by a hundred and you have the average number of junk features a set that size drags along. Divide that by how many you picked, and you have your false discovery estimate. Then try a bigger margin.

The bottom of the range sits below it — those features are selected less often in the real data than shuffled data manages by chance. Points drift above the line through the middle, and only four break away, which is why the cutoff lands where it does rather than somewhere in the crowd.

⋅ What the fixed count is worth

Step 2 pins the permuted support size at 18 rather than letting cross‑validation choose it, and the claim attached to that line is that the alternative under‑counts false discoveries. The invalid variant looks better and is worse. Free to re‑tune on permuted data, cross‑validation settles on smaller supports, the null frequencies fall, and every estimate of the false discovery count shrinks with them. Six features clear a bar that reads 0.092 — comfortably inside the target — and one of the six is noise, so the realized proportion is 0.167. It found the fifth real feature, which the published version misses. It also broke the only promise the procedure exists to make.

Roughly double the level asked for, at every level. Ask the invalid variant for 10% and it delivers 17.8%, holding its promise on fewer than half the cohorts. The trade is legible in the power column — 0.875 against 0.690 — and that is exactly the trade the paper refuses: a procedure that beats the alternative on power while failing the guarantee it is named after is not a better procedure, it is a differently‑tuned lasso with a number attached to it.
Both arms here use M = 30 rather than 100, and 40 cohorts rather than 200, so that the free‑CV variant — which re‑runs a full cross‑validation on every permuted resample instead of walking a path — finished in minutes rather than hours. Read the two halves against each other rather than against the 200‑cohort table below: the pinned arm’s power lands in the same place at either setting, 0.690 here against 0.699 there, while 40 cohorts is few enough that the last column moves by several points on resampling alone.
? Does this hold across repeated runs?

A single cohort proves nothing. The whole pipeline was therefore re‑run on 200 fresh cohorts, with a new design matrix, new outcome noise, new bootstraps and new permutations each time. That is a little over a million lasso fits. The column to watch is not the mean false discovery proportion but the last one: the share of individual cohorts on which the realized proportion actually came in at or under the level that was asked for.

The guarantee holds, and it holds with room to spare. Asked for 10%, the procedure delivers 2.6% on average. That gap is not luck: the estimator assumes every variable could be null, so Fdr^ is an upper bound and the procedure stops earlier than it strictly needs to. The cost is in the power column. At q=0.05 fewer than half the real features survive; the two weakest are usually gone.

Set against that, the CV‑tuned lasso finds 96% of the real features and reports about eleven, of which roughly five are noise. It clears a 10% false discovery proportion on one cohort in twenty. Neither column makes one procedure better than the other. They answer different questions, and only one of them lets you state the question in advance.

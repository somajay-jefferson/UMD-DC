Algorithm two

PS‑Fdr

Cross‑validated lasso does not control false discoveries. He et al. demonstrate this brutally: 340 patients, 23,052 genes, 50 of which truly matter — and across 10,000 repetitions the false discovery proportion clusters around 0.90. Nine in ten “findings” are noise.

The goal is the familiar one. Benjamini and Hochberg do not promise zero false positives; they promise that a known fraction of what you report is false, and let you choose the fraction. PS‑Fdr wants that same guarantee. What it gives up is the usual route to it — there is no p‑value anywhere below, and nothing is assumed about the sampling distribution of β^. The only raw material is how often a variable survives resampling, and that is available for any selection rule at all.

It has to be given up, because the fix cannot use p‑values — penalized estimators have no tractable limiting distribution — and it cannot use naive permutation either, because under a global null the noise variables face less competition for the L1 budget than they do in real data, so the false discovery count comes out too low. What follows is the workaround.

FDP= N0N+ FDR=E(FDP) Fdr^= e^0N+

  1. 1

    Stability selection real data

    Rather than trusting one CV‑tuned fit, ask how reliably each variable gets picked across resamples.

    for b=1,…,B B = 50
    resample n rows with replacement
    fit lasso ⟶ S(b) ={j: β^j(b) ≠0} call site A
    Πj= 1B ∑b=1B 𝟙(j∈ S(b) ) Π(1) ≤⋯≤ Π(p)

    Selection frequency, then sorted into order statistics.

  2. 2

    Permutation null calibration

    Shuffle the outcome to sever every association, then run the whole of step 1 again. Nested loops — this is where the compute lives.

    for m=1,…,M M = 100
    permute y kills all y–X association
    for b=1,…,B
    resample, fit lasso call site B
    fix the number selected to the median from step 1 — do not re‑run CV
    Π―(j) = 1M ∑m=1M Π~(j)(m)

    Averaged over ranks, not over variable identities. Once y is shuffled, “feature 12 was picked often” means nothing — the labels no longer attach to anything. What is stable across shuffles is the shape: how high the most‑selected feature gets when nothing is real, how high the second, and so on. So each permutation is sorted before anything is averaged, which is what the parenthesised subscript records.

    That fixed count is the whole trick. Letting CV choose freely on permuted data is exactly what makes the expected false discovery count collapse and the procedure over‑select.
  3. 3

    Normalize

    Borrowed from Significance Analysis of Microarrays. Divides each selection frequency by its own sampling noise — the standard‑error correction that turns a raw proportion into a signal‑to‑noise ratio.

    D(u)= u u(1−u) +ν ν=1B

    Πj is a proportion out of B draws, so its standard error is u(1−u)/B — largest at u=0.5, vanishing as u→1. Dividing by that is the whole idea: a fixed gap in raw frequency is cheap in the middle of the range and nearly impossible at the top, and D is what makes those two comparable. ν is not a fudge factor either — with B resamples no frequency can be resolved finer than 1/B, so that is the floor on the denominator, and it caps the statistic at D(1)=B. Run more resamples and the ceiling rises with them.

    Z(j) = D(Π(j)) Z―(j) = D(Π―(j))

    Real arm on the left, null arm on the right.

  4. 4

    Step down and cut

    Walk the ordered statistics until the real arm separates from the null arm by a margin, then count how many null values would have cleared the same bar.

    Z(Δ)= min { Z(j) : Z(j) ≥ Z―(j) +Δ }
    N+(Δ) = ∑j=1p 𝟙( Z(j) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many real variables clear the cutoff — the denominator.

    e^0(Δ) = 1M ∑j=1p ∑m=1M 𝟙( Z~(j)(m) ≥Z(Δ))

    How many null variables would have cleared it — the numerator.

    S^q = {j: Fdr^ (Δ(j)) ≤q }

    At q=0.1: of 10 selected, at most about 1 is expected to be a false positive.

B is resolution, not just precision. Π only takes values in steps of 1/B, so at B=10 a noise predictor needs just ten straight hits to reach Π=1. Under the null that happens constantly, e^0 never falls, and nothing clears any target. The paper’s B=50 and M=100 buy room at exactly the end of the scale where the decision is made. Even here the smallest non‑zero Fdr^ that can be expressed is 0.05/2 = 0.025.

A worked example

Everything below was produced by running this repository’s own implementation, ps_fdr.py, on a problem small enough to print. There are seventy subjects and thirty features, and five of the features carry signal. Their coefficients descend from 1.6 to 0.4, so the last one is deliberately faint. Neighbouring features are correlated at 0.35, and the outcome noise is σ=1.5, which leaves the signal accounting for 78% of the variance in y.

The design matrix is handed to the algorithm from outside, fixed, exactly as the published method assumes. That assumption is the one the composition has to give up.

Nothing is wrong with that fit. It finds every real feature, and it was tuned to do the one thing cross‑validation optimizes, which is predict. It simply answers a different question from the one being asked. Two thirds of what it reports as a finding is noise, and the fit itself gives no way of knowing which two thirds. At the paper’s scale — 340 patients, 23,052 genes — the same figure is about 0.90.
1 Resample fifty times and count

Draw seventy subjects with replacement, fit a CV‑tuned lasso, write down which features survived, and repeat fifty times. The bar for each feature is the fraction of those fifty fits that kept it.

real feature noise feature every row is one resample · every mark is a feature that survived it
This grid is the whole of step 1. Fifty fits, thirty features, and one mark wherever a fit kept a feature. Nothing else about those fits is retained — not the coefficients, not the fitted lambda, not the prediction error. The bars are the column totals divided by fifty, and they are the only thing the rest of the procedure reads.
The ordering is already most of the answer. Features 1 to 4 were selected in all fifty resamples. But feature 5, the faint real one, sits at 0.90 — and so does feature 30, which is pure noise. Every other noise feature trails below. Stability alone ranks well and cuts nowhere; the rest of the algorithm exists to place the cut.
The support sizes are also recorded here. Across the fifty resamples the CV‑tuned lasso kept as few as 8 features and as many as all 30, with a median of 18. That median is carried into the next step and never re‑derived.
2 – 3 Build the null, then put both arms on one scale

Shuffle y, run all fifty resamples again, and do that a hundred times over — five thousand further fits. On every one of them the support size is pinned at 18. The permuted frequencies are then sorted within each permutation and averaged position by position, so the null describes what the j‑th largest selection frequency looks like when nothing is real.

Both summaries, on the same picture. Each row is one permutation’s sorted frequencies; darker is more often selected. The outlined column is rank (30), and the bar beneath it is the mean of those hundred cells — 0.936, the number at the top of the null column in the table below. Summary B is the amber. Seventeen of the three thousand cells reached Π~=1.00 — a predictor kept by all fifty bootstraps on shuffled data. Fifteen sit at rank 30 and two at rank 29, spread over fifteen of the hundred permutations. Read that as a frequency: on roughly one shuffled dataset in seven, some pure‑noise predictor was unanimous. Counting them and dividing by M gives e^0=17/100=0.17, and against the four features the real arm puts at that level, Fdr^=0.17/4=0.043. The whole guarantee is those amber squares counted.

Both arms then pass through D(u) with ν=1/50=0.02. A selection frequency here is a proportion out of fifty resamples, and the standard error of such a proportion is u(1−u)/50. Dividing by u(1−u) is therefore measuring each frequency in units of its own noise, up to the constant 50 — the same move as forming a t‑statistic. The map is strictly increasing, so it reorders nothing.

The ν is doing something different, and it is worth separating out. It is there to stop the denominator reaching zero at u=1, and in stopping it, it fixes a ceiling: D(1)=1/ν=50. The two mechanisms hand over to each other near the top of the range.

The reason that matters is one line of step 4. A single scalar Δ is applied at every rank at once, so the margin has to mean the same thing at the top of the range as in the middle. On raw frequencies it does not. Below, the same gap of 0.06 at four different places:

? Does this hold across repeated runs?

A single cohort proves nothing. The whole pipeline was therefore re‑run on 200 fresh cohorts, with a new design matrix, new outcome noise, new bootstraps and new permutations each time. That is a little over a million lasso fits. The column to watch is not the mean false discovery proportion but the last one: the share of individual cohorts on which the realized proportion actually came in at or under the level that was asked for.

The guarantee holds, and it holds with room to spare. Asked for 10%, the procedure delivers 2.6% on average. That gap is not luck: the estimator assumes every variable could be null, so Fdr^ is an upper bound and the procedure stops earlier than it strictly needs to. The cost is in the power column. At q=0.05 fewer than half the real features survive; the two weakest are usually gone.

Set against that, the CV‑tuned lasso finds 96% of the real features and reports about eleven, of which roughly five are noise. It clears a 10% false discovery proportion on one cohort in twenty. Neither column makes one procedure better than the other. They answer different questions, and only one of them lets you state the question in advance.
